< When you look at this, it looks like a mess because there is algebra trapped inside a giant power of 4. To solve it, you use a core calculus tool called the Chain Rule.

Imagine you are looking at this problem on a Calculus 1 exam:\(\text{Find\ the\ derivative\ of:\ }f(x)=(3x^{2}-5x)^{4}\)

The Chain Rule is often described as the "Layers of an Onion" rule. You work your way from the outside in:

- Step 1: The Outside Layer. Ignore the math inside the parentheses for a second. If this was just \(X^{4}\), the rule of calculus says you bring the \(4\) to the front and change the power to a \(3\).
      \(\mathbf{4}(\text{leave\ the\ inside\ alone})^{3}\)

- Step 2: The Inside Layer. Now, you look only at the math that was trapped inside: \(3x^2 - 5x\). You take the derivative of just that piece.
  The derivative of \(3x^{2}\) is \(6x\), and the derivative of \(-5x\) is \(-5\).
  \(\text{Inside\ derivative}=\mathbf{(6x-5)}\)

- Step 3: Multiply them together.
\(f^{\prime }(x)=\mathbf{4}(3x^{2}-5x)^{3}\mathbf{(6x-5)}\)

Once you see it broken down, your brain will start remembering, "Oh right, I bring the power down, keep the inside the same, and then multiply by the derivative of the inside."
